## TL;DR

- **Problem and diagnosis ·** Native NVFP4 RL can become unstable late in training. We examine quantization, activation scaling, KV caches, and execution paths, then study how their errors affect updates.
- **Algorithm & engineering ·** TRIAGE controls residual mismatch risk through update direction. miniTE reduces numerical execution differences and optimizes low-precision computation. We evaluate them separately.
- **Measured gains ·** On 8×B300, miniTE with BF16 backward achieves approximately 1.33–1.37× BF16 speed for complete learner updates. A separate fixed-output serving test reaches approximately 1.64× throughput.
- **Limits and open questions ·** In the reported online RL run, weight conversion and synchronization offset sampling gains. The practical miniTE checkpoint also remains below BF16 on the five-task average. Matching operators and obtaining a small gap do not guarantee BF16-level learning; error tolerance, update rules, and final outcomes need further study.

<a id="section-1"></a>

## Abstract

Native NVFP4 reinforcement learning must remain stable under quantization and execution error while delivering practical efficiency gains. We investigate late-training instability by examining quantization formats, activation scaling, KV-cache precision, and framework differences that affect policy probabilities. TRIAGE addresses residual mismatch by adjusting updates in high-risk segments according to the mismatch and update directions, improving stability and task performance. miniTE separates quantization policy, linear layers, and compute backends, providing precision configuration, alignment checks, and performance analysis. Joint work on Transformer Engine’s per-token NVFP4 path uses contiguous layouts, device-side metadata, amax (maximum absolute value) reuse, grouped GEMM, and fusion to reduce preparation and dispatch overhead. On 8×B300, both miniTE paths with BF16 backward achieve approximately 1.33–1.37× paired speedups over BF16 for complete learner updates. A separate fixed-output serving test reaches approximately 1.64× BF16 throughput. These learning and system results come from separate configurations; lower mismatch and faster local computation do not yet establish BF16-level learning or an end-to-end RL speedup.

<a id="section-2"></a>

## 1. Introduction

Reinforcement learning for large language models repeatedly performs rollouts, policy-probability recomputation, gradient updates, and weight synchronization. Long responses increase the token volume for both generation and training, making low-precision computation valuable. Blackwell supports native NVFP4 matrix multiplication, and prior pretraining work has demonstrated low-bit training with scaling, mixed precision, and suitable rounding strategies [[1](#ref-1)]. An RL loop must additionally connect the numerical implementations used by the training and inference frameworks.

Our early NVFP4 runs improved reward initially but later developed abnormal entropy, growing training–sampling log-probability gaps, and declining reward. Quantization error, checkpoint conversion, activation scaling, KV-cache precision, and operator execution on both sides affected the outcome. Replacing a single quantization operator, or confirming that both sides used NVFP4, did not resolve all of these issues.

We first describe the failures, then use controlled experiments to identify and locate their causes. This leads to complementary algorithmic and engineering tracks. TRIAGE changes the update rule to control the effect of residual mismatch on learning. miniTE acts on numerical execution, reducing avoidable differences while turning low-precision computation into practical efficiency gains.

In a large training framework, changing scale granularity or a GEMM backend can require changes to layer wrappers, grouped layouts, communication, and autograd. miniTE separates quantization policy from these shared functions, allowing developers to replace a compute path within an existing training workflow and then evaluate its numerical correctness, learning behavior, and runtime cost.

Section 2 traces native NVFP4 RL failures and their diagnosis. Section 3 presents TRIAGE and its results. Sections 4–7 describe miniTE, implementation optimizations, experiments, and usage. We report algorithmic outcomes and system performance separately, with the configuration for each experiment.

## 2. Why Native NVFP4 RL Becomes Unstable

<a id="section-4"></a>

### 2.1 Failure Develops During Training

We run GRPO on DAPO mathematics tasks with Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-30B-A3B [[4](#ref-4),[5](#ref-5),[6](#ref-6)]. In early native NVFP4 runs, reward increased normally before training deteriorated. In the trajectories analyzed by TRIAGE, the 4B model became unstable after approximately 300 steps, while mismatch in the 30B model began growing rapidly after approximately 700 steps. Diagnosing this process requires measurements over training time rather than a single initial error value or a short loss curve.

We track reward, entropy, the training–sampling log-probability gap, and downstream task scores. For token $t$ in response $i$, define $\\delta\_{i,t}=\\log\\pi\_{\\mathrm l}(y\_{i,t}\\mid x\_i,y\_{i,<t})-\\log\\pi\_{\\mathrm s}(y\_{i,t}\\mid x\_i,y\_{i,<t})$. Here $x\_i$ is the prompt, $y\_{i,t}$ is the current token, and $y\_{i,<t}$ is its generated prefix. The learner and sampler assign conditional probabilities $\\pi\_{\\mathrm l}$ and $\\pi\_{\\mathrm s}$; $\\log$ denotes the natural logarithm. A negative gap means the learner assigns a lower probability, and a positive gap means it assigns a higher one. Mean absolute gap averages $|\\delta\_{i,t}|$ over the tokens being evaluated.

<a id="section-5"></a>

### 2.2 Quantization Error, Conversion Error, and Execution Mismatch

Quantization error compares low-precision computation with a higher-precision reference. Training–sampling mismatch compares the actual executions of the learner and sampler. Checkpoint conversion and inference-backend adaptation can introduce additional error. Before integrating RL, we therefore compare BF16 inference, online NVFP4 quantization, and inference using offline-converted NVFP4 weights.

NVFP4 represents quantized values in E2M1: E and M give the exponent and mantissa bit counts, with one additional sign bit. Decoding gives $\\hat x=q\\,s\_b\\,s\_g$, where $q$ is the E2M1 value, $s\_b$ is an E4M3 block scale shared by 16 elements, and $s\_g$ is an FP32 outer scale. The recipe determines whether the outer scale is computed per tensor, per expert, or per row.

A Qwen3-0.6B NVFP4 pretraining checkpoint initially suffered severe performance degradation because its fused QKV structure was handled incorrectly. After the fix, mean Lambda accuracy was approximately 0.3043 for BF16, 0.3056 for one-dimensional quantization, and 0.3109 for two-dimensional block-scaled quantization. Conversion and structural adaptation must therefore be checked before attributing such a failure to the FP4 format.

For Qwen3-4B, online quantization reduced Lambda-base and Math23K scores by 0.0355 and 0.047 relative to BF16. Offline conversion introduced additional reductions of 0.0089 and 0.019. These comparisons separate the loss from the format and numerical path from the additional loss introduced by conversion and scale handling.

### 2.3 Activation Precision, Scaling Granularity, and the KV Cache

With NVFP4 weights fixed, increasing activation precision tests the effect of activation quantization. W and A denote weights and activations; the following numbers give their bit widths, so W4A16 uses 4-bit weights and 16-bit activations:

| Weights / activations | Dense gap | MoE gap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| W4A16 | 0.0139 | 0.0166 |
| W4A8, MXFP8 activations | 0.0363 | 0.0227 |
| W4A4, NVFP4 activations | 0.0903 | 0.0445 |

*Table 2. Activation-precision comparisons using the corresponding unquant inference adaptation paths. This experiment and Table 1 belong to different stages of the investigation and are not a single ablation.*

W4A16 brings the gap close to the BF16 reference, identifying activation quantization as an important factor in this configuration. In another fixed configuration, changing the KV cache from FP8 to BF16 reduces the gap from 0.0721 to 0.0583. Sampler-state precision also affects cross-framework agreement.

We next examine the scaling domain. Sampler decoding processes the current token, whereas the learner typically processes a full response at once. If the activation's outer scale uses tensor-wide amax, the same token can receive different scales depending on the other tokens in its batch. Per-token amax restricts the reduction domain to the current row and removes this dependency.

Let $x$ be a reference vector and $\\hat x$ its decoded approximation, each with $n$ elements. Relative L2 error is $\\|\\hat x-x\\|\_2/\\|x\\|\_2$; signal-to-noise ratio is $\\mathrm{SNR}=20\\log\_{10}(\\|x\\|\_2/\\|\\hat x-x\\|\_2)$, in dB; and mean absolute error is $\\mathrm{MAE}=n^{-1}\\sum\_{j=1}^{n}|\\hat x\_j-x\_j|$. The notation $\\|\\cdot\\|\_2$ denotes the Euclidean norm. Relative error and SNR assume a nonzero reference vector. Lower relative L2 and MAE, or higher SNR, indicate closer reconstruction.

### 2.4 Mismatch Must Be Interpreted Alongside Update Direction

Let $A\_i$ denote the advantage of response $i$, measuring its reward relative to other responses in the same group. The figure abbreviates it as $A$ and writes a token’s gap as $\\delta$. In GRPO, a positive advantage tends to increase sampled-token probabilities; a negative advantage tends to decrease them. At synchronized weights, the gap reflects execution differences. After learner updates, it also includes parameter changes relative to the fixed sampling policy.

When the learner underestimates a token (δ < 0), a negative-advantage update lowers its probability further and can amplify the local discrepancy; a positive-advantage update tends to reduce it. The roles reverse when the learner overestimates the token (δ > 0).

Intervention therefore needs both gap magnitude and update direction. TRIAGE uses the signs of advantage and gap to identify local risk. This local analysis motivates the algorithm; actual updates also depend on cross-token coupling and the optimizer.

## 3. TRIAGE: Direction-Aware Stabilization of Native NVFP4 RL

<a id="section-14"></a>

### 3.1 Mechanism

TRIAGE operates on a policy objective that already includes importance corrections such as truncated importance sampling (TIS) [[10](#ref-10)]. It retains native NVFP4 forward computation and manages residual mismatch by modifying the optimization objective. The method combines segment diagnostics, a direction-selective gate, and bounded repair.

The gate uses mean gap and deep-tail token counts in 64-token segments to assess risk. In high-risk segments with negative advantage, it attenuates update weights only for negative-gap tokens; all other tokens retain their original weights. The weighted loss is then normalized within each response, redistributing update contributions.

Repair adds a signal for negative segment offsets in positive-advantage responses. It applies a one-sided pseudo-Huber penalty with bounded derivative to mean gaps in 128-token segments, increasing learner log probabilities in active segments. The gate reduces risky updates, while repair supplies a segment-level corrective signal. Parameters and normalization are specified in Appendix B.

<a id="section-15"></a>

### 3.2 Stability and Task Performance

TRIAGE's NVFP4 baselines already use per-token activation scaling on both sides. The experiment measures residual mismatch in that native stack. ### 3.3 Complementary Algorithm and Engineering Tracks

TRIAGE and miniTE intervene at different points in the problems identified above. TRIAGE changes updates when residual mismatch remains. miniTE directly examines and improves quantization, operators, and their invocation while reducing execution cost. The following sections address the remaining engineering questions: how to compare numerical paths and reduce their execution cost. We evaluate the two tracks separately; additional gains from combining them remain to be tested.

## 4. miniTE: Aligning Numerical Execution and Optimizing Low-Precision Compute

<a id="section-18"></a>

### 4.1 Controlling Numerical Differences and Execution Cost

NVFP4 experiments repeatedly require local changes: activation-scale granularity, higher precision for selected layers, backward formats, and quantization or GEMM implementations. When these choices are tightly coupled to model modules, distributed communication, and training scripts, each experiment must also revisit surrounding logic, making errors harder to locate.

miniTE addresses these issues at the execution layer, making quantization rules explicit and comparing the learner and sampler at matching numerical boundaries. It provides ordinary linear layers, combined LayerNorm–linear modules, and MoE GroupedLinear modules. A common quantization interface connects Python references and optimized backends. Megatron and the upper-level RL system still orchestrate training, while inference frameworks such as SGLang perform sampling [[7](#ref-7),[8](#ref-8)]. miniTE focuses on integrating, comparing, and optimizing numerical policies and operators.

### 4.2 Layered Interfaces and Transformer Engine Integration

The module layer retains parameters, autograd, and distributed interfaces needed by the training framework. The quantization-policy layer specifies formats and scales. Compute backends implement quantization and GEMM. Developers can validate numerical behavior with a reference before integrating CUDA, CUTLASS, or TE implementations behind the same interfaces.

| Layer | Components | Extension point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Module | `Linear`, `LayerNormLinear`, `GroupedLinear` | Model integration and distributed wrappers |
| Precision configuration | `RecipeSpec`, `get_qlinear_params`, `QLinearParams` | Named selection; activation, weight, gradient, and fprop/dgrad/wgrad precision |
| Quantization interfaces | `quantize`, `group_quantize` | Quantization parameters, scale granularity, padding, grouping |
| Matrix multiplication | `qgemm` and backends | Encoded data, scale layouts, GEMM dispatch |

*Table 4. miniTE responsibilities. New numerical policies are primarily implemented in the quantization and backend layers, reusing shared module logic.*

TE integration is a core design requirement. miniTE follows existing Linear interfaces and checkpoint parameter names so Megatron wrappers can replace the corresponding modules while reusing the surrounding training workflow. Parameter loading, extra state, and parallel modes are validated for each integration path to balance migration cost and behavioral consistency.

The recipe registry stores names, purposes, and dependency metadata; the parameter factory converts the selection into `QLinearParams`. The registry and inspection entry points use only the Python standard library. Operator imports are deferred to the corresponding factory branches, so developers can inspect a configuration before preparing its CUDA, TE, and compiled dependencies. Reference and optimized paths continue to share the upper-level modules.

<a id="section-20"></a>

### 4.3 Precision Recipes and Selective Quantization

The W4A16, W4A8, and W4A4 experiments in Section 2 require independent configuration of weights and activations. miniTE additionally separates gradient precision and GEMM settings for forward propagation (fprop), input gradients (dgrad), and weight gradients (wgrad). A new forward quantization policy therefore need not commit to a particular backward precision.

Version 1.0.0 selects named recipes through `MINITE_RECIPE` or `get_qlinear_params`, including `nvfp4_per_token` and `nvfp4_per_token_bf16_bwd`. Explicit layer `qlinear_params` take priority over environment selectors. Layer-name overrides and layer-number quantization exclusions support sensitivity analysis. Development can begin with reference quantization: establish scales and encoding, choose a GEMM backend, then compare full-precision and quantized backward paths.

miniTE CUTLASS and miniTE per-token are two implementations of MoE GroupedLinear. Both use NVFP4 forward computation and compute activation outer scales per token. CUTLASS names a matrix-multiplication implementation, whereas per-token names a scaling granularity; the terms are not mutually exclusive, and the TE per-token path also calls CUTLASS kernels. The main differences are below.

| Property | miniTE CUTLASS | miniTE per-token |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Activation outer scale | One per token | One per token |
| Weight outer scale | One per expert weight tensor | One per row of the flattened expert weights |
| Quantization and GEMM interfaces | miniTE packing and CUTLASS V2 grouped GEMM | TE per-token bulk quantization and grouped GEMM, with list/dense packaging |
| Recipe | `nvfp4_cursor2_cutlass_v2` | `nvfp4_per_token` |

Both paths were tested with MXFP8 backward and BF16 backward. Backward precision is configured separately and cannot be inferred from the path name. The reported BF16-backward performance uses the layer bypass, which saves the original BF16 operands and skips backward quantization. The dedicated `nvfp4_per_token_bf16_bwd` operator is a separate implementation and requires its own performance measurements.

### 4.4 Numerical Monitoring, Profiling, and Alignment Diagnostics

### 5.1 Numerical and Implementation Choices for Per-Token Scaling

We conduct joint testing and implementation work with NVIDIA around TE's NVFP4 support, using the per-token recipe and its interfaces as an important integration point [[9](#ref-9)]. Scale granularity affects numerical behavior as well as amax computation, scale storage, and GEMM epilogue cost. Accuracy and speed therefore need to be evaluated together.

NVFP4 encodes data in E2M1, uses an E4M3 scale for each block of 16 elements, and adjusts the range with an outer FP32 scale [[1](#ref-1)]. Let $s\_i$ be the outer scale of row $i$, and $b\_{i,j}$ its length-16 block. Then

$$
d\_{i,j}=Q\_{\\mathrm{E4M3}}\\!\\left(\\frac{\\operatorname{amax}(b\_{i,j})}{6s\_i}\\right),
\\qquad
\\hat x=s\_i d\_{i,j}Q\_{\\mathrm{E2M1}}\\!\\left(\\frac{x}{s\_i d\_{i,j}}\\right).
$$

Per-tensor scaling shares one outer scale across a tensor; per-token scaling computes it per row. The latter reduces dependence on other tokens' maxima and can mitigate block-scale underflow when row magnitudes differ greatly. It still retains within-row dynamic-range and FP4 rounding error, and requires finer-grained compute support. Public systems also combine per-token NVFP4 forward computation with higher-precision backward computation [[2](#ref-2)].

We retain multiple recipes and evaluate numerical choices separately from implementation optimizations. Lower local quantization error alone does not establish more stable RL training. The cost of additional reductions is likewise evaluated over the full computation path.

### 5.2 Contiguous Layouts and Grouped GEMM Invocation

Original grouped interfaces may split quantized data and scales into separate expert tensors, then reconstruct pointers, strides, and layouts for GEMM. As expert counts increase, Python/ATen slicing, list construction, temporary allocation, and host-to-device metadata transfers can offset FP4 arithmetic gains.

We organize quantization outputs into contiguous buffers and describe expert boundaries with offsets, passing them directly to dense grouped GEMM. FP4 data, block scales, and outer scales travel through the same interface, avoiding splitting after quantization and reassembly before computation. Device-side metadata construction and workspace reuse reduce allocation and synchronous copies. Zero-token experts and padding are handled within the same grouped path.

This optimization changes data organization and invocation. Dense quantization and GEMM interfaces are checked against the original list path for operands and outputs; performance is measured for the combined implementation. Revision `b641179` later combines the dense path, GEMM settings, and fusion. The core repeated tests retain their original revision identity.

<a id="section-26"></a>

### 5.3 Computing amax Where Data Are Produced

A separate amax kernel first scans activations, after which the quantizer reads them again. We look for operations that already traverse the data along the producer-to-consumer path.

For MoE FC1, inputs pass through dispatch and permutation, allowing rowwise amax to be accumulated during token reordering. Padding processes activations and amax together, so quantization can reuse the external statistics and skip repeated reduction.

For MoE FC2, amax is better fused with activation computation. We use `_weighted_swiglu_row_amax_kernel` as the fused kernel, following the JIT-compiled implementation and accumulating amax while traversing columns. The statistic applies to the SwiGLU output after router weighting, exactly the tensor passed to FC2 quantization. Reusing the existing memory and computation path avoids another complete scan of the FC2 input for amax.

The FC1 transformation is:

```text
Original: sort → pad(activation) → amax → quantization
Fused:    sort + amax → pad(activation, amax) → quantization
```

FC2 produces amax together with weighted activations and reuses it in subsequent quantization:

```text
Original: SwiGLU × router weight → activation → amax → quantization
Fused:    weighted SwiGLU + row-amax → activation, amax → quantization
```

The benefit must cover the producer, padding, and consumer. Module-level simulations that skip amax are not always faster, so eliminating one kernel does not by itself establish a complete-flow benefit. Section 6 uses learner timings that include surrounding work.

<a id="section-27"></a>

### 5.4 Kernel Optimizations and Their Scope

Kernel-level optimization addresses cross-CTA amax reduction, FC1/FC2 GEMM pipelines, scale-layout transfer, and activation/amax fusion in the epilogue. The implementation details and applicable scope follow.

<details open>
<summary>Expand: Kernel-Level Implementation Details</summary>

**1. Row-amax: cluster shared memory for cross-CTA reduction.** The K1 stage of per-token NVFP4 reduces amax across K-tiles for every row. The original path combines tile results with `atomicMax`. In the optimized path, CTAs first compute partial amax and write to the leader CTA's shared buffer. The leader reduces within the cluster. A cluster covering all K-tiles directly produces final row-amax; larger ranges fall back to atomics. In the recorded tests, `n_cluster=16` and `k_tile=128` cover K=2048, while increasing the tile to 256 covers K=4096. This replaces only K1 amax; K2 cast remains separate. The multi-cluster scheme showed no benefit and was removed. The optimization applies where a single cluster covers all or most of K, without extrapolating its benefit to larger K.

**2. FC1/FC2 GEMM: choose tiles and pipelines by reduction length.** Both layers use grouped NVFP4 GEMM, but their K lengths and epilogue-overlap conditions differ. FC1 has a longer K and MMA loop. `MMA_N=256` with `2SM` increases work per CTA and gives epilogue work more opportunity to overlap MMA. FC2 has a shorter K; reusing 256 columns with `OverlappingAccum` can make epilogue reads compete with MMA writes for the next stage. It therefore uses `MMA_N=128` and `2SM`. TMEM has 512 columns. A two-stage 256-column tile fills that capacity, leaving no room for scales and requiring an OverlappingAccum strategy instead of default TMEM allocation. These are configurations for the tested FC1/FC2 shapes; the optimal choice varies with shape.

**3. Fused swizzle: write the GEMM scale layout directly during cast.** Downstream GEMM requires a specific swizzled scale-factor layout. The original implementation produces compact M-major scales, then launches `nvte_swizzle_scaling_factors`, adding a kernel launch and global-memory traffic. The fused implementation adds a `with_swizzle` path to `launch_grouped_fused_cast_bf16`, writing the cuBLASLt 128M × 4K tile layout directly in `DO_ROW`. When disabled, the compact layout and separate conversion remain available. This eliminates intermediate scale reordering while retaining the quantization formula. Subsequent miniTE per-token tests found bit-exact agreement in quantized payloads, scales restored to the same layout, and final outputs between fused and unfused paths.

**4. Activation and epilogue fusion: compute gating and statistics at the FC1 output.** MoE FC1 produces paired gate/up tensors, usually followed by SwiGLU or GeGLU and output-side amax for subsequent quantization. The fused epilogue performs alpha/bias correction, paired gate/up activation, router-probability weighting, and D-side amax. Depending on the configuration, it preserves optional writes of the pre-GLU intermediate C; the FP8 path can also generate output scale factors. The core dataflow is:

```text
ACC = block-scaled GEMM(A, B)
ACC = ACC × α_a[m] (optionally × α_b[n]) + bias
C = gate | up (pre-GLU, width N; optionally written)
D = act(gate, up) × prob (SwiGLU/GeGLU, width N/2)
Reduce D amax concurrently; FP8 can generate output scale factors
```

Computing activation and statistics in place reduces writes and subsequent reads of complete gate/up intermediates, together with separate activation/amax kernel launches. This fusion currently targets forward paths in which FC1 immediately feeds gated activation and quantization statistics. Inclusion in the main performance configuration is recorded separately for each optimization; complete benefits remain based on the corresponding module and learner timings.

### 5.6 Exploring Single-Pass Fusion

We also investigate online amax compensation for fused SwiGLU-FFN paths that avoid materializing the full intermediate activation. FC2 consumes activation K-tiles before the full-row amax is known. The method maintains a running rowwise amax and converts earlier partial accumulations to the new scale whenever the outer scale increases, connecting FC1, SwiGLU, quantization, and FC2.

This path primarily reduces intermediate-activation memory traffic and separate kernel calls. Compensation maintains a consistent scale when accumulating tile contributions; the E4M3 block scales and E2M1 codes produced earlier still depend on the scale and rounding used at that time. We therefore retain it as a fusion research direction, evaluating compensation error, quantization differences from two-pass execution, and the complete call-chain benefit separately. This prototype is evaluated separately from the main B300 learner configuration in Section 6.

<details>
<summary>Expand Online Amax Compensation Quantization</summary>

#### Algorithm: accumulate FC2 partial outputs in the current row scale

Let $P\_i$ denote the SwiGLU activation for FC2 K-tile $i$. Complete the FC1 reduction for the corresponding gate/up slice, then compute SwiGLU, the rowwise absolute maximum, and the maximum of each 16-element block. The running rowwise amax and outer scale are

$$
g\_i=\\max\\bigl(g\_{i-1},\\operatorname{rowmax}(|P\_i|)\\bigr),\\qquad
gs\_i=\\frac{g\_i}{2688},\\qquad 2688=6\\times448.
$$

For block $b$, the quantizer produces $bs\_{i,b}=Q\_{\\mathrm{E4M3}}\\bigl(\\operatorname{amax}(P\_{i,b})/(6gs\_i)\\bigr)$ and $q\_{i,b}=Q\_{\\mathrm{E2M1}}\\bigl(P\_{i,b}/(bs\_{i,b}gs\_i)\\bigr)$. FC2 block-scaled MMA computes

$$
\\Delta O\_i=(q\_i\\odot bs\_i)\\,\\widetilde W\_{2,i},\\qquad
O\_i=O\_{i-1}\\frac{gs\_{i-1}}{gs\_i}+\\Delta O\_i,
\\qquad Y=O\_k\\,gs\_k\\,gs\_{W2}.
$$

Here, $\\widetilde W\_{2,i}$ includes the weight block scales but excludes the outer weight scale $gs\_{W2}$, which is constant along K. The rowwise activation scale and outer weight scale are broadcast over their corresponding output dimensions. If the outer weight scale varies by tile, incorporate that variation into each partial instead of applying one common factor at the end. Activation `bs` is applied block by block inside MMA; only the outer `gs` is deferred to the epilogue.

#### Numerical conditions: avoiding saturation does not imply invariance for every gs

For fixed data, $gs\\ge\\operatorname{amax}(P)/2688$ keeps the unrounded block scale within the E4M3 maximum of 448. The online running amax covers the current tile maximum, so the current tile satisfies this condition against upper-range saturation.

Without E4M3 rounding, $bs\\cdot gs=\\operatorname{amax}(P\_b)/6$, allowing the block scale to cancel an outer-scale change. In practice, rounded `bs` leaves a scale-dependent rounding residual in this product; a large `gs` may also push small blocks into the subnormal or underflow range. Power-of-two rescaling can preserve the decoded step size when the codebook stays in the normal range, avoids its boundaries, and uses consistent rounding. Arbitrary scale ratios do not have this guarantee.

The original heavy-tailed example uses one activation row with amax approximately 29. The design scale and four times that scale both yield rel-L2 0.099; reducing the scale to one quarter of the design value raises rel-L2 to 0.52:

| Outer scale | rel-L2 | Block-scale upper-range behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| amax / 2688 | 0.099 | 1/16 reaches 448 exactly, without exceeding it |
| 4 × amax / 2688 | 0.099 | No block reaches the upper limit |
| amax / (4 × 2688) | 0.52 | 6/16 exceeds the upper limit and is clipped |

This example illustrates the role of the saturation condition and equal error under the specified scale multiplier. It does not establish identical codes or errors for every scale above the lower bound.

#### Compensation: merge partials in one common outer scale

$\\Delta O\_i$ has not yet been multiplied by its tile's $gs\_i$, so partials from differently scaled tiles cannot be added directly. The recurrence converts the old accumulator to the current scale. Expanding it in ideal real arithmetic gives

$$
O\_i=\\sum\_{j\\le i}\\Delta O\_j\\frac{gs\_j}{gs\_i},\\qquad
O\_k gs\_k gs\_{W2}=\\sum\_j\\Delta O\_j gs\_j gs\_{W2}.
$$

The identity restores the outer scale of each already-quantized tile. It does not recompute earlier `bs` or `q`, and therefore does not establish bitwise equivalence to two-pass quantization with a known full-row amax. FP32 ratios, multiplication, and accumulation also round and must be checked in numerical comparisons.

The original diagnostic uses two slices with amax 1 and 100 and an FP32 reference. Directly adding differently scaled partials yields rel-L2 2618%. Restoring each slice's scale before summation and using sequential ratio compensation both yield a recorded rel-L2 of 6.0%, within the quantization-error range of this example.

| Merge method | rel-L2 (%) | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Add both partials directly | 2618 | Mix outputs represented in different outer scales |
| Restore each gs, then add | 6.0 | Restore each tile's actual contribution |
| Running-scale ratio compensation, then restore gs | 6.0 | Perform the same scale conversion recursively |

#### Implementation flow and overhead

The fusion prototype targets SM100 (Blackwell) through CuTeDSL. FC1 accumulates the corresponding gate/up slice in TMEM, then transfers it to registers for SwiGLU, absolute rowmax, and quantization, with running amax stored in a workspace. FC2 directly consumes E2M1 data and E4M3 block scales. Its epilogue retrieves partials from TMEM and compensates and merges them in registers or SMEM. The core flow in the normal numerical range is:

```text
Inputs: X, W_FC1, W_FC2
Initialize gamax = 0, gs_prev = 0, O = 0
for each FC2 K-tile i:
    Complete FC1 reduction for gate/up slice → SwiGLU → P_i
    rowmax = max(|P_i|); gamax = max(gamax, rowmax)
    If gamax = 0: this row contributes zero; skip computation
    gs = gamax / 2688
    Generate E4M3 bs and E2M1 q per 16-element block
    FC2 block-scaled MMA → ΔO_i (includes block scales, excludes gs)
    O = O × (gs_prev / gs) + ΔO_i
    gs_prev = gs
Y = O × gs_prev × gs_W2; all-zero rows output zero
```

SwiGLU outputs can be negative, so rowmax must use `max(|P|)`. Explicitly set `q=0` for all-zero rows or blocks to avoid `0/0`. If a nonzero block's scale underflows, apply the quantizer's zero-value rule and record the associated error.

This path avoids writing and reading the full FP32 intermediate activation, but `O` must remain outside TMEM, adding partial transfers and a merge for every K-tile. In the supplied B200 Qwen3-30B-A3B shape-test record, the prototype is 4–8× faster than the unfused TE two-level chain, with accuracy close to that path. Against the fused single-level path, merge overhead is more visible for a 512-token small batch, while the gap at 4096 tokens is approximately 5%. Prefill and larger batches are therefore useful workloads for further evaluation. This prototype shape test is measured separately from the complete B300 learner tests in Section 6.

</details>

The multi-stream, multi-tensor path still performs repeated algorithm selection and prepares shapes, layouts, and call parameters for expert problems. This is the CPU bubble to examine in that path. The counts directly support increased preparation and scheduling work; the duration of GPU gaps still requires the corresponding timeline.

**Dense grouped GEMM targets this preparation and scheduling chain.** Section 5's contiguous buffers, device-side offsets and metadata, and reusable allocations put expert problems on one grouped scheduling path, reducing host-side slicing, list assembly, argument construction, and repeated organization. These changes affect the work surrounding GEMM and the complete call time, so the observed difference should be interpreted together with the implementation path. ### 6.6 What the Experiments Establish

Together, these experiments explain miniTE's implementation choices. Operator and gradient checks support interfaces, layouts, and backward precision. Module forward and direct F+B timings locate local costs. Real-text learner tests establish whether these choices reduce complete update cost. Serving and online RL tests reveal the additional constraints of weight conversion and synchronization.

Low-bit training benefits must therefore be validated at multiple levels: numerical correctness, sustained learning, faster local computation, and manageable integration cost. We retain core measurements and complete configuration comparisons at each level, using miniTE test records for performance results.

### 7.1 Integrating Existing Models through Linear Interfaces

The implementation package is `miniTransformer`, and the linear layer is miniTE's basic integration unit. `Linear`, `LayerNormLinear`, and `GroupedLinear` provide ordinary projections, combined normalization and linear computation, and MoE expert computation. Existing Megatron wrappers can connect to these modules, or users can pass parameters constructed from a named recipe directly:

```python
import torch
from miniTransformer.module.config import get_qlinear_params
from miniTransformer.module.linear import Linear

params = get_qlinear_params("nvfp4_per_token")
layer = Linear(
    1024, 1024,
    bias=False,
    params_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
    device="cuda",
    qlinear_params=params,
)
```

Recipe selection determines the numerical path; module integration determines which model layers use miniTE. The MoE launcher sets both for its miniTransformer backends. `GroupedLinear` imposes output-dimension and grouped-layout constraints and does not support sequence parallelism. Integration should check the specific model and parallel configuration.

miniTE retains TE/Megatron checkpoint parameter naming and compatibility interfaces. The current extra-state interface does not restore all TE internal quantization state. Parameter-loading compatibility and full internal-state compatibility are validated separately.

## 8. Discussion: From Numerical Alignment to End-to-End Learning

The experiments address low-precision RL through two tracks: TRIAGE changes updates in the presence of mismatch, while miniTE reduces execution differences and compute cost. Neither resolves every issue. The next questions are whether faster computation yields end-to-end savings, and whether closer numerical agreement produces better learning.

<a id="section-47"></a>

### 8.1 Weight Updates and Synchronization Remain a Bottleneck

Here, `update weight` means converting, transferring, and loading the trained weights into the sampler. This remains a major bottleneck in the configurations we measured. Faster learner updates and rollouts do not imply a proportional reduction in complete RL iteration time. In the run reported in Section 6.5, conversion and synchronization costs offset the gains from faster sampling.

Further optimization should consider parameter representations, quantization and packing, transfer, loading, and synchronization frequency together. Possible directions include reusing low-precision representations within the same weight version, combining conversion with transfer, and overlapping synchronization with sampling under explicit version control. Less frequent synchronization can save transfer time but increases policy staleness. These choices should be evaluated using complete iteration time and task performance under matched training budgets. Online amax and deeper operator fusion also need evaluation within this end-to-end scope.

<a id="section-48"></a>

### 8.2 Why Does Low Precision Remain Sensitive After Operator Alignment?

Matching operators and quantization recipes removes some execution differences. Matching their names alone, however, does not guarantee identical floating-point computation across input shapes, batches, and parallel configurations. Floating-point addition is not associative: changes in reduction order, fusion boundaries, or tiling can change the result. A fixed execution path can be deterministic. The remaining question is where perturbations arise when execution conditions change and how they affect subsequent updates.

Our alignment experiment and long-run evaluations expose two sides of this problem. The strict alignment configuration reduces gap to approximately 10⁻⁸, while miniTE’s long-run learning and checkpoint evaluations do not match BF16 in every comparison. These results use different configurations and measurement scopes, but together they show why numerical agreement and final learning quality must be evaluated separately. Why can seemingly small perturbations be more damaging in the low-precision configurations we tested?

Candidate mechanisms include persistent error bias, rounding transitions near coarse quantization boundaries, MoE routing changes, and the accumulated response of gradient clipping and optimizer state. These remain hypotheses. More informative experiments would hold the model and data fixed, control the location, direction, and correlation of perturbations, and compare subsequent updates and long-run reward in BF16 and low precision, rather than relying on a single-step average gap.

<a id="section-49"></a>

### 8.3 Optimize Updates and Final Learning Outcomes

Mean log-probability difference cannot be converted linearly into reward. Similar average gaps can occur on different tokens, advantage signs, and response segments, with different effects on updates. A small gap also does not guarantee useful exploration or gradient signal. TRIAGE’s directional analysis offers one starting point. Further work can directly examine how errors alter updates, testing corrections through reward and evaluation scores under matched data, update, and wall-clock budgets.

The community’s approaches are broadening too. Together AI’s Score Centering (SC) adds a correction that removes mismatch-induced drift from the policy gradient and can be combined with importance sampling [[17](#ref-17)]. The Qwen team’s TRACE uses rollout-side quantization outcomes to guide training-side FP4 rounding and compresses the guidance information [[18](#ref-18)]. SC changes the gradient estimator; TRACE coordinates quantization decisions. TRACE still targets alignment, but both works expand the available interventions beyond matching operator implementations.

For miniTE, this means supporting joint experiments on update rules, precision allocation, and sampling policy alongside controlled, comparable numerical execution. We hope the community will investigate how to preserve task-relevant learning signal, suppress bias that compounds across updates, and translate these changes into end-to-end gains. The goal is better learning within a given resource budget, not simply a smaller consistency metric.

## Appendix B. TRIAGE Method Conventions

We use response-level normalization from the release manuscript: compute a weighted token-loss mean within each response, then average across responses. With valid-token mask $m\_{i,t}$ and detached gate weight $w\_{i,t}$,

$$
\\mathcal L\_{\\mathrm{PG},i}
=\\frac{\\sum\_t m\_{i,t}w\_{i,t}\\ell^{\\mathrm{base}}\_{i,t}}
{\\sum\_t m\_{i,t}w\_{i,t}},\\qquad
\\alpha\_{i,t}=\\frac{w\_{i,t}}{\\bar w\_i},\\quad
\\bar w\_i=\\frac{\\sum\_t m\_{i,t}w\_{i,t}}{\\sum\_t m\_{i,t}}.
$$

The fixed gate uses 64-token segments. For negative-advantage segments, the mean-tier weight is 0.1 when mean gap is below −1.5, 0.3 for [−1.5, −0.5), and 1.0 otherwise. If $k$ valid tokens in the segment satisfy $\\delta<-6$, the tail weight is $1-0.9\\min(k/2,1)$. The smaller of the two segment weights is applied only to negative-gap tokens. Other tokens retain weight 1.

Repair uses 128-token segments within positive-advantage responses and applies a one-sided pseudo-Huber penalty beyond the negative boundary. Masks, segment normalization, and derivatives are described in the TRIAGE method appendix and main method section.

Component ablations branch from the full-TRIAGE step-300 checkpoint and compare continued training. The complete figure is in the TRIAGE ablation. These are continuation comparisons rather than ablations trained from scratch.

The diagrams and interactive presentation draw on *The 4-bitter Lesson* and the LMSYS Blackwell low-precision RL article [[15](#ref-15),[16](#ref-16)]. Constructed examples and conditional calculations are identified separately.
